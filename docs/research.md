# Research and editorial method

This is a curated introduction to the history of philosophy, with an emphasis on Western traditions and additional global context. Inclusion is selective. A count of entries is not a ranking of importance, and the label “Western” describes this site's emphasis rather than a sealed intellectual boundary. Islamic, Jewish, Asian, African, and European traditions have exchanged texts and arguments over many centuries.

## Source status

The initial HTTPS proxy blockage was resolved during this session on 1 October 2026 for the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (SEP) and Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy (IEP). Full reference pages were retrieved, and relevant philosophical, biographical, and bibliographic passages were read. The 52 Western entries now contain 62 inspected SEP/IEP links, including supplementary biographical articles for movement sequences. `Source.verified: true` means the linked reference text was inspected for relevant facts; an HTTP 200 alone was never treated as verification.

This is a curated, source-informed introduction, not an exhaustive scholarly reference. A verified article does not automatically substantiate every precise endpoint or editorial interpretation. Individual notes retain important limits. Marx's London residence is a conventional historical fact that is not established in the inspected general SEP article; supplementary Britannica retrieval still returned proxy 403, and attempted IEP biography paths returned 404. The map therefore retains only his familiar London interval, marks it approximate, and discloses this verification limit. Several other precise geographical or publication claims were removed or replaced with source-supported, sparse activity markers rather than claiming a complete itinerary.

Source comparison matters: SEP gives Plato's birth as 429? BCE, while IEP discusses the familiar 428/427 convention; SEP gives Seneca's birth as c1 BCE rather than the often-used c4 BCE. The Camus IEP work list gives 1943 for The Myth of Sisyphus, while SEP explicitly describes publication in October 1942; the dataset follows the latter. The current SEP Habermas text explicitly reports 1929–2026. These choices and alternatives are disclosed in record notes.

The global and event datasets have their own source notes and retrieval-status flags. Named citations point to relevant resources; they do not imply that every editorial coordinate, window, or concise formulation is directly asserted by a source. A `verified: false` flag, if present in any dataset, still identifies an uninspected reference.

## Dates and works

All stored years follow **astronomical year numbering**: `1` is 1 CE, `0` is 1 BCE, `-1` is 2 BCE, and `-399` is 400 BCE. A BCE label therefore uses `1 - year`, not the absolute value of the stored number. This makes chronological distances consistent across the BCE/CE boundary. Display labels should avoid showing a historical “year zero.”

`datesApproximate` marks uncertain life dates. It does not mean every year in the record is approximate. Ancient birth dates and some traditional chronologies are particularly uncertain. `Work.approximate` separately marks a work's dating. A single work year may mark the first volume, an edition's title-page year, a composition endpoint, a lecture, or a posthumous publication; `dateKind` and the note explain which. Multi-volume dates are not collapsed without a note.

Some ancient treatises have no secure composition year. Their approximate display years place them broadly within a period and must not be presented as exact events. Aristotle's treatises, Epicurus's letter, and Heraclitus's fragments are examples. Socrates, Thales, and Zeno have no dated authored works listed where surviving or inspected evidence cannot establish a defensible work date; Plato's testimony is not a Socratic publication. Plotinus's Enneads and Weil's Gravity and Grace are posthumously arranged collections, rather than books prepared in that form by their authors. Plotinus's display marker now records the approximate end of treatise composition in 270, with Porphyry's later arrangement explicitly explained.

Unknown life dates remain unknown. Heraclitus is shown by a floruit around 500 BCE: `birthYearUnknown` states that the numerical birth field is only a chronological activity anchor. Parmenides's approximate birth follows a disputed inference from Plato, and his death is unknown. `deathYearUnknown` distinguishes such historical uncertainty from a currently living person. `activityEndYear` supplies a coarse, source-based activity-window endpoint for navigation, not an estimated death year or invented lifespan. Display such records with “fl. c.” or a question mark, as appropriate.

Familiar bibliographic dates can differ from release years: Locke's Essay and Two Treatises use 1690 title pages although they appeared in 1689; Schopenhauer's first major work is dated 1819 but was released in late 1818. Descriptions preserve these distinctions. Entries list representative works first; ordering is an editorial choice rather than a bibliography.

## Geography and reception

Locations are selected residences, teaching appointments, principal civic settings, or expressly identified single-year events. They are not a continuous itinerary. A philosopher may have no marker during an unrecorded interval. An interval marked approximate can include travel; its note says what it represents. Descartes's frequent Dutch moves and Nietzsche's seasonal journeys are not converted into fictional continuous residences. Foucault's Paris residence while commuting to Clermont-Ferrand is distinct from his teaching institution. Arendt's forced exile and education are represented through source-supported intervals, with gaps left visible. Single-year markers for Marcus Aurelius's accession and Sartre's public lecture likewise do not assert permanent residence.

City coordinates use modern approximate city centers. They locate a historical setting, not a documented house or lecture room. Modern country names help navigation and must not be treated as contemporary historical borders. Hippo Regius is placed near present-day Annaba; Königsberg uses the location of present-day Kaliningrad.

Influence regions are **qualitative reception contexts**. They carry no intensity, measured influence score, exclusive boundary, or causal strength. The regions are deliberately coarse: Europe, the Mediterranean, North America, East Asia, or global reception. Their displayed time windows are editorial summaries of broad periods, not dates on which a doctrine suddenly arrived or disappeared. An endpoint does not claim the philosopher ceased to be read. Modern windows ending in 2026 simply extend to the current dataset date.

Reception claims summarize reference discussions of later schools, readers, translations, or debates. `confidence: 'medium'` expresses the coarse interpretation and geography rather than a statistical confidence level. Continuing scholarly reception is extended through 2026 where the reference supports it; finite ancient-school intervals describe that historical context, not the subsequent disappearance of an idea. A record may have no regions when the inspected material does not provide a defensible geographical reception claim.

Every region note distinguishes the qualitative claim from measured spread. A map should label this view “qualitative reception,” should show the period and notes, and should use the same visual treatment across regions rather than implying a computed heat level. Residence and reception must remain distinct views.

## The three interpretive lenses

Coordinates range from -1 to +1 on three editorial axes: matter to mind, experience to reason, and individual to collective. **These are interpretive lenses, not objective measurements, doctrinal equivalences, or a source-derived classification system.** The main question lane is also a navigation choice; each thinker addresses more than one question.

Each coordinate has its own concise rationale. A zero can mean a mixed position, a doctrine that resists the axis, or a question on which the selected work supplies little basis for classification. It does not mean a philosopher occupies a measured midpoint. Numerical distance must never be read as measured similarity or an amount of influence. The view is a prompt to read the explanations, not a substitute for them.

Several simplifications require particular care:

- Descartes's dualism affirms both thinking and extended substance; a positive mind coordinate does not make him an idealist. Spinoza's monism is not equivalent to either materialism or subjective idealism.
- Aristotle's matter–form account is not reductive materialism. Early Stoic corporealism is also different from modern mechanistic materialism.
- Kant's transcendental idealism coexists with empirical realism. Husserl's phenomenology changes across his career, and bracketing an assumption does not mean denying it.
- Hume's skepticism is not a positive materialist ontology. Peirce's realism about generals and James's radical empiricism resist an easy matter–mind binary.
- Reason and experience are not mutually exclusive methods. Empiricists use reasoning; rationalists engage empirical science. These placements summarize emphasis, not the absence of the other method.
- Hobbes's sovereign, Rousseau's general will, Hegel's ethical institutions, Marx's class emancipation, and Arendt's public action are distinct accounts of society. A collective coordinate does not equate them or identify a present-day party position.
- Mill's liberty coexists with impartial concern for welfare. Wollstonecraft and Beauvoir connect individual agency with social reform. Anscombe's virtue and action theory do not reproduce all of Aristotle's ethics.
- Early and later Wittgenstein must be distinguished. Kierkegaard's pseudonyms voice different positions; the speakers in Plato's dialogues likewise cannot all be treated as direct statements by their author.
- Nietzsche's perspectivism is not simply “anything is true.” His unpublished notes and subsequent political appropriation must not replace the published works. Heidegger's Nazi commitments require explicit historical attention when discussing his philosophy.
- Later Marxist movements and state policies are not interchangeable with Marx's own writings. Colonial participation, racism, sexism, and exclusions in canonical thinkers must not disappear behind concise summaries.

The data format allows richer contextual notes and additional citations as verification proceeds. Representative figures, chronology, locations, and sources should be revised when evidence warrants it rather than preserving visual symmetry.

## Validation performed

The Western dataset was checked for unique IDs, complete coordinate rationales, coordinates within [-1,1], plausible life and work ordering, posthumous publication flags, sorted residence periods within known lifespans or documented activity windows, valid latitude/longitude ranges, and HTTPS citation URLs with explicit retrieval status. Relevant reference passages were separately inspected as described above. Structural checks do not replace historical source review, and inspection does not turn editorial coordinates into measured facts.
